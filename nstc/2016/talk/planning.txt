## Blurb: "The Art of Questioning to improve Testing, Agile, and Automating"

*Blurb: "The Art of Questioning to improve Testing, Agile, and Automating"*

Asking questions is essential for effective decision making. Unfortunately much of our decision making is based on belief rather than fact, because we don't ask the right questions. Questions run through our entire software development process process. Testing can be modeled as a 'questioning' activity where testers identify assumptions and presuppositions in a system, and use those to create test ideas and ask questions of the system during exploratory testing. Automating can be modeled as a 'questioning' activity where we codify questions and expected answers. In this talk Alan will provide practical examples of asking questions to improve Agile processes and approaches to Automating. Alan will also provide a model of questioning that attendees can use. A model that management can apply to help practitioners improve, even when managers don't fully understand the technical details of the task. Attend this session to learn how effective questioning helps people take responsibility for improving their process.

But there is a danger of becoming dependent.

Particularly when you ask questions of a consultant. Then it could become expensive.

Agile is a whole bunch of attributes.

The ability to:

- respond to change
- delivery quickly
- release quickly

etc.

How do we even recognise change? - asking questions.

How do we deliver quickly? - building the minimum necessary without risk.

We have to ask questions to identify what the minimum is, are the risks acceptable, what is the aim? how will it work?

Are the 'acceptance criteria' good enough? Are they complete.

I get very nervous when I see 'agile' processes that don't ask a lot of questions. Where 'acceptance criteria' are accepted without a lot of questions, and without a lot of 'how will we know', and without assessing the risks. Risks go on to the back log or are 'accepted'

I get nervous when people don't share their decisions, and their answers, because it often leads to unconditional acceptance of a 'conversation' that people were not part of and 'dependence' that 'other people' know what they are doing and what they want to achieve.

This 'dependence' or 'acceptance' is decision making based on belief rather than fact.

Agile needs a lot of questions.

Automating can be modeled as a 'questioning' activity where we codify questions and expected answers.

The questions that we ask in an automated way we expect to have the answer 'yes'.

The only time the automated work gives us 'new' information is when it fails.

Pretty much we ignore CI builds, when they are green. We expect them to be green.

And if we  have done the work to codify the 'right' assertions and checks then we have a good safety net. If we have gaps in our assertion coverage, then that is a hole that bugs might fall through and not be caught, and our safety net isn't as good as we thing.

We have to get good at asking questions. And continuing to review - are we asking the right questions. Are we asking enough questions.
