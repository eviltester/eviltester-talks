This talk was a Keynote at TestWorksConf in Amsterdam. Some coverage of the history of automation, but hopefully from a practitioner focused point of view than a management one.

I do think that with the best teams I've worked with, the things that made them great were not just their technical ability - that is clearly important, but I think it is somewhat of a platitude to keep saying it. When are performing technical tasks, the more technical ability we have, the better we will perform them. That's a given.

But the people and teams that worked best also had the ability to model what they were doing, and understand that there are multiple layers and views of that model. They didn't necessarily have the concept of abstraction as I've tried to explain it here, but they did have some sort of modelling concept even if it was reduced to concepts such as 'domain model' or 'page object model', and we were able to work as a team to refactor our work using concepts such as 'dry' (don't repeat yourself) and single responsibility to expand the abstractions that we had, without necessarily having to teach the team the concept of abstractions.

But since this was a Keynote, I wanted to try and get across the high level concepts that drive the type of work that I do.

And abstractions are a big part of that.

I think that we need the ability to respond effectively and contextually to demands, and that falls under the "Requisite Variety" concept from Cybernetics, which I find an enormously important concept and seems to form the backbone of most forms of psychotherapy that I've studied, even if it isn't stated as the backbone, and normally isn't stated as the backbone, but I view is as a common theme between fairly discrete therapies.

In psychotherapy it boils down to - people respond the way they do, because those are the responses they have, but in some situations those responses are detrimental, so if we can help people build new responses, then we can help them. And very often the therapy process is a process of building new responses, giving the client new options. And that can be viewed as increasing their requisite variety.

I think if people want to work with the needs of their current context as their prime driver, then that means they need to have a range of responses to call upon and have a response that works in context, not in a generic, or an absolute sense. And that range defines their 'requisite variety'

And of course both of those concepts 'Requisite Variety' and 'Abstractions' fall under our ability to model and respond within Systems.

Learning to see systems everywhere, and from different angles has been an enormous help to me.

So when I look at the development process, I don't just see a process, I see a system. A System of Development, and then then allows me to question how it fits in with other systems. And also to apply all the tools I have for working with systems to my model, or abstraction, of this system.

When we view things as systems, we can then put our abstractions, and the assumptions that those abstractions imply, to the test. Either by asking questions, or by changing our response (utilising our high requisite variety to respond in a different way) and observe the result. If we don't see a different result from our system, but we were expecting to, then our model may be wrong.

I think these concepts are important for management was well. But for practitioners, if we understand these concepts, even as simple instantiations, then we can improve.

So simple instantiations:

- Abstractions
    - page objects, libraries rather than frameworks, domain models, DSL rather than Gherkin
- Requisite Variety 
    - multiple ways of reporting, multiple ways of using the same tool (i.e. JUnit is not a unit test tool, it is a test execution framework), multiple tools to do the same thing, etc.
- Systems
    - individuals as systems
    - teams as systems
    - 'processes' as systems
    - ourselves as systems
    - and actually study systems based topics - systems analysis, systems thinking, cybernetics, etc.

And that's it.

I didn't realise, when I started building the talk that those three concepts were going to come across so strongly, but when I narrowed down the talk to focus on the notion that it isn't "Automate or Die!" that is the problem, its that we haven't worked out how to survive in that environment with flexibility, and people haven't read any of the history associated with 'automation' where I think those three concepts shine through.

But now you know, what to go and research, and look up, to automate the systems that you work with.
