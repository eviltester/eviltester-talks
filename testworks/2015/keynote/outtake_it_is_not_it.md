Slide 30: "Whatever you might say something "is", it is not"

"Whatever you might say something "is", it is not"

You will find an aversion to "is"ness in more places than Korzybski's work. I just use that as an, easy to quote, example. 

Albert Ellis, the man who created Rational Emotive Behavioural Therapy advocated e-prime. E-prime meaning "English without the verb to be". And Ellis wrote several books in e-prime to attempt to make his writing as clear as possible.

But Ellis had studied the work of Korzybski so I need to find a better example.

"Ceci n'est pas une pipe" Apologies for my French pronunciation.

Rene Magritte's painting of a pipe, with the words "Ceci n'est pas une pipe" below it.

The writing translates to "This is not a pipe".

And of course he had painted a pipe, he so clearly the wording described it correctly.

"This is not a pipe". More accurately "This is a painting of a pipe with a description under it of the words "Ceci n'est pas une pipe"" But for some people "This is art" might convey their perception of it more accurately.

And psychologists have categorised biases that can apply in this circumstance- "Functional Fixedness" where someone perceives a thing as having a single and can't see beyond that use. And they fight to retain that bias, because that abstraction or that bias, has meaning to them beyond what the abstraction itself suggests. And they face the danger that their 'functional fixation' may limit their requisite variety, because they only see one response and they remain fixed on it.

In terms of a pipe, a functional fixedness would result in "a pipe is for smoking". For me, "a pipe is for blowing bubbles", because I once owned a small plastic pipe in my formative years, and I used it for blowing bubbles.

And the concept clearly didn't appear in the 1920's with Magritte since Zen has a proverb that covers similar ground.

I read this in "The Japanese ARt of War" By Thomas Cleary, and the proverb says

"This is it, but if you fixate on it, then it isn't anymore."

Meaning that enlightenment exists in 'it', but our awareness of 'it' prevents us from experiencing the enlightenment. And our abstractions around 'it' make 'it' not 'it' anymore, it has become 'it' with all our fixations, and abstractions associated. Leading to one interpretation of Zen's emptiness as a lack of fixations and abstractions, opening us to the possibilities of reality now, rather than the abstractions we bring to it.

And all of that, simply to say, that Korzybski's words "Whatever you might say something 'is', it is not" have a long lineage. And that provides some social proof, but also, a trail of previous and related work that we can mine for our own purposes.

And when we become aware of our abstractions, we hopefully become aware of our biases and fixates, and can increase our requisite variety.
