### How they relate

I used a few books and papers to help prepare for the talk:

- "Automation" by Carl Dreher
- "Automation" by John Diebold
- "Beyond Automation" by John Diebold
- "The Economic Consequences of Automation" by Paul Einzig

All of the above are out of print, but available relatively inexpensively from on-line retailers.

I had not delved into the history of automation prior to this talk and my initial starting point was "Automation" by Carl Dreher. Primarily because I loved the cover with a cartoon bearded end of day prophesier bearing a placard "Automate or Die". A very slim book that I enjoyed reading, and from it I learned of Norbert Wiener's aversion to the word, and the origin of the word (John Deibold).

I bought "Automation" by John Diebold to try and fact check Dreher's assertion of the spelling issue. And indeed, that is mentioned in Deibold's book. But Deibold's book also had a lot of useful quotes and information that demonstrated to me the historical providence of many of our currently discussed issues.

I bought "Beyond Automation" after researching Deibold and it treads much of the same ground as "Automation" but had some pithy quotes and was equally well written.

I found "Economic Consequence of Automation" in one of the books, and bought it to have a historical view of a 'money' focused look at automation. I do not view automating from a money view, but so many people do that I wanted to understand the historical relevancy. It was very much an economics book and I didn't take much from it. But if you work in an organisation that does demand ROI metrics etc. then this might help you.

I could not find an authoritative source to back up Dreher's claim of Norbert Wiener's "barbarous" view. But I did a search and found "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation" which is available on-line. And I from there I saw his lack of usage of the word automation. The paper follows on from Wiener's book "Human Use of Human Beings", which also describes some of the controversies associated with automating processes that humans are involved in.

Immediately prior to the talk I re-read "Experiences of Test Automation" with a view to 'would I recommend it to the audience'. And while I didn't take much out of the book on initial reading, I found a good overlap with my presentation and although I didn't agree with some of the conclusions in the book, I could recommend it as a source of 'real world testing' lessons learned related to automating parts of different projects test processes.

After the talk, I discovered on-line the book "Automation and Society" which is a collection of essays written around the time of "Beyond Automation" and includes an essay by Deibold. This book is probably the easiest introduction point to gain a historical perspective on the topic of "Automation".

During the talk I mentioned "Experiences of Test Automation" by Dot Graham and Mark Fewster.
