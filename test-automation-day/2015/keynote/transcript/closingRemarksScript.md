## Closing Remarks

I'm just going to tell you one of my learning secrets, just in case it wasn't obvious from this video.

When I want to study management, I tend not to read the newer books on management.

We seem to have drifted in to a world where a book, or a talk, or any kind of article can only have 1, 3 or 5 main points. When that is the case I can probably read that book in about half an hour, so I don't gain a lot of value from most modern management books - there are some exceptions, but they are rare.

Older management books however, are much more suited to my style of learning. They tend to just dump out a massive amount of material on the reader. So I gain much more from reading those. I also learn that the problems we face are not as new as we think.

And so I read more older management books than modern, and I do keep hunting through archive.org and project guttenberg for older texts I can read.

So I suggest that whatever books you read that you gain value from, you also look through the bibliography and references and see what books they mention, and then read those and repeat: go to the bibliography and references, and find the books they built on, then repeat. When you do that you will encounter books that none of your peers have read, that are possibly viewed as old fashioned, but you will find information in it that other people have forgotten, and that can give you an edge in the workplace.

This is something I do, and I know that I am never ever going to run out of great books to try and hunt down.

For modern management books I tend to read them via safari books online because then I don't feel too bad if the book doesn't have much in it of value. And for the older books I find value in, I then try and hunt down a paper copy -even though it might take me a few years to find an affordable copy.

So keep studying, and do mix in a study of your craft's history with a study of its up to date instantiations.
