Resources to support "Push Your Functional Testing Further" talk presented at March Sigist 2016.

### Systems Theory

I showed an image of Ludwig Von Bertalanffy's General System Theory. Which discusses why we need to look not just at the single parts and processes in isolation, but also to view them as a System in how they can be organised and find order within the whole.

It mainly deals with 'organic' systems, since he was a biologist, but that is good, because not only does it discuss feedback, it also discusses attributes that we might miss if we focus on software systems:

* growth
* competition

Growth - how will the system 'grow' over time with data? How will we add to it as it expands in terms of maintenance or extensibility?

Competition - what other systems does it compete with in live? Does it live in harmony with them or does it compete for the same resources? 

You can skip the math, there is a lot of goodness in this book, I find it very readable.

The references will also lead you to Ackoff, Ashby & Wiener and into the land of Cybernetics.

### Precondition and Presupposition

My application of precondition and presupposition into testing started after reading:

* The structure of Magic (vols 1 & 2)
* The hypnotic Patterns of Milton Erickson (vols 1 & 2)

All of the above by Bandler and Grinder.

Essentially - how does a psychotherapist know what to ask and what to target to effect a change in a patient?

You can derive this also from the work of Virginia Satir, Fritz Perls, Paul Watzlawick and Steve de Shazer. i.e. 'big names' in therapy - particularly the system focussed and brief therapies.

Recommended books from the above (in addition to 'magic' and 'erickson'):

* Changing with Families (Satir)
* Conjoint Family Therapy (Satir)
* The language of Change (Watzlawick)
* Words were originally magic (Shazer)
* In and out the Garbage Pail (Perls)

### Compilers and Interpreters

IF there is one topic that I am very thankful that I studied in some depth at university (primarily on my own, my course didn't cover this), it was compilers and interpreters.

The references I still keep on my shelf are:

* Introduction to Automata Theory, Languages and Computation by Hopcroft and Ullman
* Compilers: Principles, Techniques and Tools by Aho, Sethi and Ullman
* Structure and interpretation of computer programs by Ableson and Sussman
* Writing Interactive Compilers and Interpreters by P.J Brown

Of these "Compilers: Principles, Techniques and Tools" is probably the 'most classic'

You can find some MIT lectures on the topic at [MIT](http://ocw.mit.edu/courses/electrical-engineering-and-computer-science/6-035-computer-language-engineering-sma-5502-fall-2005/index.htm)

Of course, you don't need to go as heavy weight as the above books.

If you pick pretty much any "writing compilers and interpreters in <insert your favourite language here>" you will probably be fine.

I travelled around the USA on a summer holiday while at University with "Writing Interactive Compilers and Interpreters" by P.J Brown - a very simple overview which I still enjoy reading today (plus you can pick it up cheap).

I also had "Writing Compilers and Interpreters: An Applied Approach" by [Ronald Mak](http://www.apropos-logic.com/books.html). Lots of examples in 'C' since that was my language of choice at the time.
