### The Testing Illuminati

When I was a little baby tester, I was looking forward to the day when I would be initiated into the occult secrets of testing. The secrets that would let me crash the system and find defects with a few well chosen keypresses. Or the magic words that I could say in meetings that would cause everyone involved to reassess the system and identify the defects before we even start.

That day never came. I don’t know if it was because those secrets don’t exist, or I never met anyone that could initiate me, I don’t know how many of you are part of that secret and magical testing illuminati.

But I do know that the NLP Meta Model is the closest I have come to finding a model that allow me to get closer to identifying how those secrets might be found. Because it is a contextual questioning model that deals with ambiguity. And that’s why I want to bring it to your attention, and give you enough information so that even after this brief introduction you can start using it in your testing.

### NLP stands for Nero Linguistic Programming

NLP is NLP (Neuro Linguistic Programming) not NLP (Natural Language Processing). Natural Language Processing deals with computers understanding humans. Neuro Linguistic Programming deals with humans understanding humans.

## Equivoque

Software Development is communication of beliefs, Communication is ambiguous, Ambiguity leads to defects, Ambiguity leads to trance, Magician’s choice

### Joseph Weizenbaum created Eliza

Eliza is a computer program, created by Joseph Weizenbaum, for AI research, that takes human sentences, then bounces back a transformed sentence based on that one, to simulate a theraputic communication. And there is a story associated with the Eliza program that Joseph Weizenbaum came out of his office one day and found his secretary in tears because the system was so sympathetic to her problems. Now obviously the computer wasn’t sympathetic to her problems, but she was able to take ambiguous statements from the computer, internalise a meaning and react to them. Because people are great at dealing with ambiguity, or rather we are great at believing that someone else understands us when we talk.

### Communicate Ambiguous Beliefs to computers

Software Development is a human communication activity. We take the stakeholders’ beliefs about the software, which we normally call requirements, and then communicate them along a chain of humans until some of those humans communicate a representation of those beliefs to the computer in a form which the computer can turn into ones and zeroes.

And human communication is an ambiguous activity. We can generally deal with ambiguity fairly well, because there are very few occasions in our life when the ambiguity will kill us. And we often spot ambiguity and ask questions to help resolve it.

But we have to be careful about ambiguity when the chain leads to something which will not interpret our information. And if we communicate ambiguous statements to someone who will communicate it to someone one else who will… then we build in ambiguity.

Ambiguity in Software Development can lead to requirement defects, functional defects.

## Meta Model

Clients &lt;-&gt; Therapists, Language Patterns, Emotional Epiphany, Transformational Grammar, Other Models ‘What can go wrong?’ ‘WWWWWH’. Deletion Distortion Generalisation, develop skills around this model, requirement sentence

### 2 Guys in the 70s Studying Therapists

The NLP Meta Model was constructed by two guys in the 70’s, one a linguist and one a computer programmer. They wanted to study the language used by effective therapists because there were therapists that were able to create an emotional epiphany in their client with a few well chosen words.

So they listened to the statements that the client made, and the statements that the therapist said in response, and the back and forward exchange that went on. And built a model of the language that they called the Meta Model.

### Transformational Grammar

It was built around a communication model from transformational grammar, where the things we say (surface structure) are a transformed representation of a richer model of the world (a deep structure).

In the NLP meta model the transformations are classified as deletions, distortions and generalisations.

- Deletions are things removed from the deep structure, possibly some of which are important.

- Generalisations are things that are grouped together, possibly with the wrong domains.

- Distortions are things which are changed slightly, changed ever so slightly to skew the surface structure from what is actually modelled.

So I could say “I am happy to be here” (a surface structure) and that obscures a lot of the detail of the situation, heart rate, pulse, breating, what I can see, how I got here, how I actually feel (this may not normally be the state that I associate with happiness), and all of this may be different from what the word happy implies for you, etc. etc.

So in a therapy sense, they looked at the surface structure statements that a client said. Looked at what patterns were there, looked at what the therapist said in response, matched the response to the patterns, and followed it all through.

We can spot this process of transformation in all our communication. Including our written communication in program specifications and program code.

In the transformational grammar model, ambiguity is defined as being when a surface structure can be mapped back to two different deep structures.

### The Other Models

Before I learned the Meta Model I had two other models that I used to apply to documentation:

- What can go wrong?

- What, When, Where, Why, How, Who (WWWWHW)

The ‘What can go wrong?’ model is too vague, it doesn’t tell me a process of how to apply it, and it relies purely on my experience. It tells me a direction that I want to go in, but that is all. Using this model told me a lot about my experience, but nothing about how to improve my understanding.

WWWWHW is slightly better, but gives me no guidance as to when to apply each question. And consequently I may a lot of questions to identify information that might be useful. It is still very much experience based.

The Meta Model is contextual. Based on the surface structure presented. I have a set of patterns of deletions, distortions and generalisations that I can look for, and then ask questions based on those.

### How I approach Documents

When I go through requirement documents and specifications, I mark out what I find in those documents, in terms of the meta model categories.

I go through the whole document fairly quickly to build up a better model of what the document is telling me, and then I start asking questions based on the ambiguities that are still present in my model.

There are obvious ambiguities here that I suspect are probably documented in a spec somewhere.

Deletions associated with ‘The daily trade file’ what format? Where does it exist? What is it called? Who creates it? What trades does it contain? Etc. etc.

We could go on and on and this maps back to the WWWWHW model, I can use that model if I want to explore the deletions and get more information. But I’m not just using that model because I can target the cause effect statements which the WWWWWH model doesn’t really lead my to targetting. Does the system always process the daily trade file when it starts up?

Presuppositions are the things that are going to kill this requirement though.

## Generalizations

So we want more detail about Generalization.

### Adding special cases

“Have you ever worked on a system where there was an area that was being changed throughout your testing with more and more special cases getting added into the code and degrading the structure of the system?”. That might be the result of testers identifying exceptions for a generalisation.

The one that we are most familiar with and the one that we encounter all the time, is the universal quantifier. This is a statement that is universally true.

In system terms a violation always means a special case that hasn’t been identified. And if it isn’t violated then it means that testing have a lot of work to do across the scope of what is universally quantified and we better find ways of limiting the scope.

Universal Quantifier is something that we target by finding an exception.

### Limiting Rules

Modal Operators are statements that present excepted rules. The can’ts, won’ts, shouldn’ts and mustn’ts. We could accept these rules at face value, or we can find out where they come from. The rule may not be as all encompasing as is implied so we want to find out the limits of that rule.

You get these rules in systems “The system will not allow the user to…” “The system must not …” and the questions we are given by the meta model. “What stops you?” “What would happen if you did?” lead us to ask those questions of the system, because if the rule isn’t enforced under all cirumstances then we might hit a fairly severe bug. Or “Well what if you wanted to?” might show that the rule is too rigid.

### The annoyance of TPI Generalizations

The generalizations that always annoy me, are when I encounter them in the documented test processes. Where there has been some Test Process Improvement exercise that has resulted in a process full of Always, Every, All, where there are no exceptions for appropriateness.

So every test will always be documented in the test management tool with expected results and test data and a test script before execution. And the tester Must put the script in a test suite before the script can be executed. Which is all very thorough. But doesn’t take into account the context that we are working in, can stifle creativity and may well demotivate testers. Any time I encounter demotivated testers I always look for generalizations in the test process.

### Presuppositions

But the most important item here for testing is the presuppositions. If you take one thing away today, take away this. This is fairly close to “What could go wrong?” but we have an approach for finding out what could go wrong.

With a presupposition we ask “What has to be true for this statement to be true?” Then challenge and test that truth. If it isn’t true then it starts to break down the truth of the whole statement. And if we take the presupposition down a couple of levels “What is presupposed by my presupposition” then we can usually get closer to identifying “The Test”.

### Presuppositions will probably kill this example

“The daily trade file must be processed”, presupposes that it is processable, which pressupposes it exists, and what does that mean, that it has been written completely and closed. We don’t need a lot of knowledge and experience to work down a chain of inference and identify 4 or 5 negative tests in there.

Obviously we can probably do fairly well with presupposition analysis on systems we have a lot of domain knowledge about, but even when we don’t we can do a pretty good job of identifying ambiguities where the presupposition isn’t confirmed and we may have to run a test to find out what happens if that presupposition is not true.

There are a few presuppositions in the given requirement:

The daily trade file must be processable.

- What if it isn’t?

- What if it is corrupt?

- To be processable it must exist

    - What if it doesn’t exist?

The daily trade file is produced and processed once per day.

The system starts up

- What if it doesn’t?

- The system starts up once. It is the daily trade file.

    - What if it doesn’t?

        - Is the file moved after being processed, or does it sit there waiting to be processed.

The file exists, then the system starts up.

- What if the system starts up as the file is being brought into existence?

Here, I’m looking at the presuppositions, to get the tests that are really going to make a difference.

I find presuppositions to be enormously important when I’m testing.

### Exploratory Testing Presuppositions

And if I’m doing exploratory testing then I typically translate what is in front of me on the screen into a statement that I can apply to the meta model. And when I’m doing exploratory testing I do a lot of pressuposition analysis.

When I’m faced with a screen – what is presupposed by this screen?.

By the way you could if you wanted turn these statements into predicate calculus and then look at the various terms that are present if you really wanted. There exists a file such that it is the daily trade file and system start up implies that time to process &lt; 5 seconds. And then do normal proof type things on it.

There Exists – what if there doesn’t? That kind of thing. And if you do know predicate calculus, then have a look at the meta model from that perspective and you’ll see a lot of overlap. But that’s an aside – I actually find this a lot easier to use than predicate calculus anyway.

## Deletions

So, what have I not mentioned yet?

Have you ever been caught by a bug in a part of the system that you didn’t even know existed?

I was testing a system the other day and the spec said “Pressing the delete key will remove some of the items from the tree”. Because it is obvious which ones will be removed right? Well not to me, so that was a deletion that I targetted and go “which items will be deleted?” “well, X, Y, and Z”, And that gets agreement around the table that we delete X Y and Z. OK so now I have a combined surface structure synonymus with a generalization. “The system must always delete X,Y and Z”, so what about P.” 2 voices “No, not P”\|”Well Yes P” the voices of the spec and the requirement.

Now there is nothing special there. There is nothing that you wouldn’t do right. It is common sense yes? Well, it wasn’t found in the drafting, and it wasn’t picked as an omission during the review process. It was picked up by the tester. Because this is what we do. And the Meta Model gives us another way to frame what we do. And that is why I find it useful.

## Distortions

We often find distortions in human speech. And so they are useful for a test manager to find out.

Testers should be wary of using distortions in their communication, and at taking offence when other people use them.

Distortions in defect reports can really build up resistence in the development teams.

## Roots Of NLP

OK, I can slow down a bit now. I’ve covered the most important parts.

I now just want to give a little overview of NLP in general.

The Meta Model was the first part of NLP to be developed, and is documented in The Structure of Magic Vol 1.

There were a few key therapists that we know about that were modelled for that book.

Virginia Satir we are all familiar with. Well, I say that. Perhaps we are not. Virginia Satir gets mentioned in the testing literature, probably because of her influence on Jerry Weinberg and his subsequent influence on some of the more prolific American writers about testing.

And that is a good thing. Jerry Weinberg’s books are amonst the best books you can read in the IT world (generally), and specifically if you want to see what applied NLP can look like. And although I know that Jerry has studied NLP, and Gestalt and Virginia Satir, I don’t know how much of it is specifically brought in to his work. But if you read ‘Rethinking Systems Analysis and Design’ or ‘Are Your lights on?’ they are particularly relevant to what you’ve been learning today.

Virginia Satir is probably most famous for her models of change, certainly that is what I have seen referenced most in the testing literature. But if you ever get a chance to see her in action on Video then what she is really good at is working with people with compassion and directness, getting them to explore how they feel about a certain situation.

Fritz Perls was the figure head behind Gestalt Therapy. Famous for sitting people in a chair and making them hallucinate conversations and various other things. But what Fritz was really doing was smoking himself to death. When we go back and study the work of these people we just take what is useful from their work. We don’t need to take on their bad personal habits.

No, what Fritz was really doing was getting the client to explore their representational model from different aspects, and filling in the deletions in the deep structure through a process of psychodynamics, and focus on the Now part of their model. At least that’s my generalisation and interpretation of what he did.

He used a lot of interesting language and Fritz Perls was the first therapist that the NLP boys studied in detail because Richard Bandler was editing Fritz Perls’ last book.

Farrelly was a fun therapist, and he is still around if you ever want to train with one of the original influences on NLP. And Frank Farrelly introduced a lot of challenge into the theraputic process because he used a notional called provocative therapy. Where he would bring the client face to face with their beliefs about the problem and ridicule them. Because we often take ourselves too seriously and what we do too seriously and if we can get used to taking it less seriously it is probably easier to change it and adapt it.

But NLP has a Voracious modelling appetite, taking what works, figuring out models of how to make it work for someone else, and then using the model to make a similar effect.

Go back to the roots to identify nuances around that work.
