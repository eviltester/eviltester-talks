## “The Uncon'”

Key Points:

Fortean Times Unconvention 1994

“Believers” weaved their data and evidence into explanatory stories

Charles Fort collated reports of anomalous phenomena

Fort's explanations were tongue in cheek

Good: data, evidence, sources, investigability, repeatability

I can easily get explanations wrong, then people dismiss the good data.

.

In 1994 I went to an unconference. Wikipedia tells me that the term unconference was first used for an XML conference in 1998. Pah. In 1994 I went to the Fortean Time Unconference. Now know simply as “The Uncon”

Fortean Times, for those that don't know. Is a UK magazine that deals with the world of spooky.

It gets its name from Charles Fort, for those that don't know. Charles Fort was a writer in the early 1900s who researched anomalous phenomena. Stuff that didn't fit in to the scientific theories of the day.

And he catalogued lots of reports of fish falling from the sky, and black rain, and strange sea creatures, Whatever. Anomalous phenomena abounds.

I'm sure Charles Fort wrote tongue in cheek because when he collates a bunch of stuff that doesn't have a theory. He'll just make one up. Just for that set of examples. A theory unrelated to anything else. He doesn't attempt to unify his theories. He just splats them down on the page and lets them stand for themselves.

And Fortean Times does a similar thing. It reports on 'incidents', preferably with spooky photos, and documents the witness statements and whatever other 'data' or 'evidence' they have. And sometimes the writers will put forward theories around the event. But they have no 'unified' theory for the madness that they report.

And their conference gathers together people who do put together unified theories, conspiracy hunters, and ancient astronaut hunters, etc. etc.

So you go to their talks and listen politely as someone with a world view, very different from your own, presents their data, and their evidence and their story of their unified theory.

These are like the defects that don't get fixed. The one offs. On Tuesday at 14.23 I clicked on file open and got a blue screen of death. I logged in and was spontaneously logged out again.

Much as I enjoyed the uncon.

I try not to be like the Uncon presenters. I try not to draw a massive web of conspiracy correlation to explain my bug reports.

But I'm also cautious that I present enough data to allow investigation. Charles Fort would create the whackiest explanation that he could to surround the data and the evidence, if it wasn't specific enough to lead you to investigate in the correct place.

I'm wary about explaining defects. You know the 'therefore the registration process is broken and the user cannot register' part of your defect report.

So I try not to explain the conclusions. I document the data, I present the evidence, I describe the phenomenon, and I extrapolate the impact. Determine whether it is repeatable and investigable. How can we make the phenomena manifest again.

I avoid explanations as much as possible because explanations can be wrong. And if you get your explanation wrong, no-one takes your evidence seriously.

ASSAP is now a UK government recognised professional body. So now if you want to be a paranormal investigator in the UK you can get professional indemnity insurance through them and align yourself with their code of ethics for investigating the paranormal.

Through them we went on Vigil.

A vigil is where a group of you (about 15) go into a place that has had previous anomalous phenomena reported, and you vigilise.

So basically you sit in the dark for 30 mins and wait for something spooky to happen.

Terribly exciting.

And you have to get tooled up for this.

Cue: lessons learned about test tools.

Because you're all working in the same environment so you have to be conscious of the work that other people are doing around you.

So there was me, Mr Junior ghost hunter. Looking around at the other new vigilisers. Feeling pretty smug because “I had a chair”

Or with holsters.

I remember two specific parts of the vigil. One in a room, and one on the stairs.

So in the room, two of us inside. And one person outside. So we're collecting data from multiple perspectives.

I remember making notes on noises that I heard through the wall. Little torch goes on. Scribble scribble. Nothing else happens. Except I'm on edge, with that spooky cold feeling. Wanting to turn around but knowing that my chair will squeak.

And on the stairs. I was on the top. One person in the middle, and one on the ground floor.

And at the end of the vigil sessions we all get together to debrief, to go over the notes we've taken. Compare the times with other people's times and see if anything we recorded was anomalous, or if we could explain it there and then.

So the noise through the wall. When we all got together, that ceased to be anomalous. That was in fact one of the other groups shifting themselves around their vigil room. Ugh, data contamination. We had synchronised times when each vigil was supposed to start and finish. Apparently they started late.

I learned that it was important to figure out what else was going on.

The stairs however was different.

Spooky.

## E-prime

Key Points:

English without the verb “to be” (mostly)

Avoid the General Semantics identified issues: “is” of identity, “is” of predication

[http://www.compendiumdev.co.uk/eprimer/eprimer.php](http://www.compendiumdev.co.uk/eprimer/eprimer.php)

I write eviltester.com in e-prime (mostly)

I write defect reports using e-prime (always)

re: “problem with definitions”

.

e-prime advocates written English without the verb “to be”

I do not know how it translates into other languages. Which may make it a less appropriate topic for an international conference such as this. Tough.

e-prime spun out of General Semantics.

Count Alfred Korzybski in General Semantics describes some issues with the verb 'to be'. The 'is' of identity “I am a tester”. Creating the exclusive and nothing else situation where testers can't do coding because they 'are' testers.

And the 'is' of predication “testing is great”, “this is a bug” - according to who?

So David Bourland offered up e-prime. Get rid of the verb. Get rid of the problem. Extreme, but what the heck. Works for me.

I have a list of Discouraged and 'Allowed by e-prime' on my office shelf in front of me.

And about 4 years ago or so when I started using e-prime I wrote an e-prime checker for my website where I can paste in text and it can tell me what I've done wrong. In terms of e-prime violations.

[http://www.compendiumdev.co.uk/eprimer/eprimer.php](http://www.compendiumdev.co.uk/eprimer/eprimer.php)

I use e-prime whenever I raise a defect report. Every one, I write, I write in e-prime.

And most of my blog posts, I write in e-prime.

And I'm violating it tremendously in this presentation.

Because... the verb to be is great for generating emotional responses, but not always at the thing you want.

When I write a bug report I want an emotional response, but I want it based on the extrapolation of the impact. Not when I say “this is high severity” “this is a bug which must be fixed now”

E-prime forces me to write in the 'active voice'. Did you ever get told to write in the active voice by your teacher. I did. And e-prime offers a simple short cut for doing it.

So you get to write your bug reports as if you were a pulp author.

E-prime forces me to write from an observer point of view. Which makes it perfect for test logs and defect reports because it makes the report all about the observations and the actions taken. E-prime helps me avoid the temptation for explanation because I have to write “I think” instead of “I saw” or “I did”

I love it. I couldn't do my job without it. E-prime is great, for me.

But it does mean that I have a problem with definitions.

“Testing is ...” hmm can't do that.

So I end up focussing on what we do, and what the system needs done.

“When I Test, I …”

“On projects I tend to ...”

etc

so I try not to say that “I am a tester” I try to say “I test stuff”. “What is testing?” do you mean “How do I test stuff?”

This has the benefit for me that I focus on the context. What needs done now, here, on this project. And not “What is testing?” because the later moves the process of testing out of context and into the generic world of generalisation where all things become true and possible.

Why confuse things. Focus on the now of testing. The Tao of Testing.

## Requisite variety

Key Points:

Ross Ashby : “Only variety can destroy variety”

Ross Ashby === Control

Stafford Beer: “Only variety absorbs variety”

Stafford Beer === Viable Systems

Increase your flexibility and adaptability

Beware the system that adapts to make “lethal attenuation” viable.

.

This has helped me simplify my test process.

A definition of requisite variety, because I love definitions:

Ross Ashby : “Only variety can destroy variety”

Stafford Beer: “Only variety absorbs variety”

Ashby's definition came about because they were studying control and regulation and stable systems.

Stafford Beer's work uses the notion of viable systems, and clearly you would have to define viable for that to have a real context. So assume viable means 'continues to exist'.

When I first read about this it seemed simple. The more variety I have the more I can cope with my environment. So I value flexibility and multiple approaches and broad skill sets. That seems to follow on naturally. And to do that I keep my descriptions simple, I'll try and describe the inputs: the risks, or concerns, or objectives and then the approach that I plan to take to 'absorb' those and what will result as the output of that absorption: what we hope to learn, how we will describe our results.

And retain the flexibility to change the absorption approach as required.

So my strategy for surviving in big procedural systems is to give them the information in the basic formats that they are used to, while leaving me the wriggle room I need to get my job done.

Seemed simple when I first learned it. But I had some anomalies I couldn't explain

So like Charles Fort I collected them, then pondered.

I like studying various forms of psychotherapy and reading therapy transcripts etc. So why does a patient, who stares into space all the time, not respond to therapy, clearly only with one response, staring into space, when the therapist does exhibit variety?

Stafford Beer is quoted as saying that “The lethal attenuator of variety is ignorance”

Why do test approaches that are clearly inappropriate for their environment survive. Where there is a lot of failure demand, people are complaining, everyone is complaining, where documents are never signed off, where testing is never integrated with the project, massive overrun on timescales etc. etc.

So my explanation was that I had modelled the system incorrectly.

In terms of therapy the system I had modelled of the therapist and the patient, and the variety of the responses in the relationships between them. Was the wrong system. The system was really the broader system, the health care system. Where the patient was taken out of society (one system), because their responses were not viable in that system, and put into a 'home' or a 'hospital', a system where their response was viable, and worse was required. Healthy responses in the hospital system mean that you leave. So the bigger system absorbed the variety and took action to control the situation, which led to the the small system of therapist and patient not needing to change because theirs was a viable set of responses and reactions.

I was looking at the therapist and patient, not the therapeutic environment and the patient and the therapist. The bigger system had built up to make the variety the patient put out the very thing that was allowing them to survive.

With the test approaches, the bigger system, expected these flaws. The viability wasn't about meeting the needs of the stakeholders, the viability was based on sticking to budgets, or maintaining the power base of the management. Other departments used the problems to obscure their own inadequacies and their own project overruns and failures. The whole thing was viable because the function of the organisation was to engage in a giant blame game. Or the function was to report on the inefficient use of resources.

The are a mountain of reasons why these things continue even though they don't work and should not be viable. But the fundamental explanation I have relates to the encompassing system changing so that the poor behaviour becomes viable. e.g. Government IT projects, what would be the worst overriding system to encompass your IT project - politicians.

Now I would love to believe in conspiracy theories for this. I would love to create a giant web of convoluted reasons and machinations where there is a small cabal conspiring and controlling. Sadly, most of the time it comes down to incomptence. And that saddens me.

So I have 2 survival strategies.

I tend to not stick around in environments where I see this type of thing. Because I don't want to use the response that is required to remain viable within it.

I focus on the simple system, the one which I am actively involved with.

## Utilisation

Key Points:

From the work of Milton Erickson, not Aikido

work with the patient that you've got

work with the environment you have

work with the system you have

use the feedback you get from the system you are in

.

The last one. I promise.

Utilisation for me comes from hypnosis. Particularly the work of Milton Erickson.

And it relates to a bunch of things:

work with the patient that you've got. Not the one you think you should have. Operate in context of this person now in front of you. Not what they were last week, because if you're doing your job properly as a therapist they will have changed. Work with them as they are now. Use their language patterns to communicate because they will respond to those. Use the examples that they provide to demonstrate the limits in their world view.

In hypnosis if you are putting someone into a trance and you're going through your induction and you want to say “sink deeper into the quiet” and a fire engine goes screaming past with its siren blazing, don't ignore it “enjoy the quiet”. Use it “as the siren fades away, relax ever deeper”.

Harness this stuff.

Do you test the system that you have? Or are you busy testing the system you thought you were going to get?

This is an important question in Agile projects when the system is built incrementally and you thought that this story was going to have error handling and it doesn't appear to. Are you going to check? Are you going to test for error handling even though it clearly isn't working? Are you going to take the opportunity to explore it without error handling and see what the impact is if you can get stuff through – because that might give you a set of things to try and exploit later when error handling does come in.

If the system is starting to run slowly because you've been testing for a while and now have lots of data in there. Do you moan that you can't do your work because the system is too slow? Or do you use this opportunity to look for bugs when the system has been in operation for a few years and has lots of data? Do you start looking for the really slow queries? Do you start overlapping them so that multiple users are doing big queries?

I find I hard to put a limit on this one thing that I took from Milton Erickson.

It has enabled me to free up and extemporise, and improvise with my testing. And to do it entirely within the context of the application. I'm not looking outside for techniques or approaches, I'm working entirely with what the system is telling me.

The system populates the form with defaults. Have you used them? The system has instructions on the screen – have you followed them?

Working in the now, with what the system and environment and project has. Rather than what we want it to have.
