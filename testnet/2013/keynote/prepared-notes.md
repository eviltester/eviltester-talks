# Prepared Keynote Notes

Prepared script and supplementary ideas for My Quest for Silver Bullets at TestNet 2013. Original wording and paragraph order are retained. These are preparation notes, not a transcript of the delivered keynote.

## “My Quest for Silver Bullets”

My name is Alan Richardson. I blog at eviltester.com

And I am going to tell you about the lessons learned from my lifelong quest for Silver Bullets and why you should quest for Silver Bullets too.

Test Automation is often looked upon as a “Silver Bullet” and its in the theme for this conference. But the thing is that anything can be a silver bullet. Exploratory Testing, Context Driven, Model Based, Certification, Maturity Models. And it isn’t necessarily the fault of the people creating them. It can be our fault when we procure them and buy into them.

Thing is I take it for granted that automation is part of my test process. I think I work with software, so I will use software to make my software process better, and since I can write software, I’ll write software to help me with my process. So I’m going to come at Silver bullets from a slightly different angle and try in pull tales from my experience to highlight points.

And I’m going to use all the “Silver Bullet” jokes and metaphors before anyone else has a chance to. Bwahaha!

## “Silver bullets are good”

Because “Silver bullets are good”.

Is that contentious?

I've spent my life looking for Silver bullets.

There is nothing intrinsically wrong with Silver Bullets.

I started my career Building silver bullets. Without Silver Bullets I probably wouldn't be working in testing.

Silver bullets are good.

## “Silver Kills Bacteria”

Silver is a pure metal. My Son was given a Silver teething ring when he was a baby. Not by me. I’d have melted it down and made silver bullets, but by someone we know with money.

If you are born with a silver spoon in your mouth then that is a good thing and you should maximise the value of that.

Does everyone know Doc Savage? One of my favourite pulp heroes. And Doc wasn’t born with a silver spoon, he was his father’s silver bullet: trained from an early age in science and experimentation and physical fitness. An he uses his genius and herculean strength to fight crime.

This is “Death in Silver” where a bunch of thugs dress up in Silver costumes and leave a trail of destruction in their successful attempts to steal millions of dollars. And in this particular book the head bad guy dies, so Doc doesn’t get a chance to use his particular Silver Bullet. Despite being a paragon of perfection, even he wasn’t immune to thinking that he had found a perfect solution.

Doc’s approach to ‘curing’ criminal tendencies. Was a form of lobotomy, where he operated on the criminal to remove their criminal tendencies and then hypnotised and brain washed them into being productive members of society again.

## “The lone ranger, used Silver Bullets”

You can’t get a better endorsement than this.

Ignore the fact that the Lone Ranger is a fictional character.

The lone Ranger used Silver Bullets. This is good.

Silver bullets are pretty common in fiction. Remember this.

Silver Bullets are often great for solving problems that don’t exist. We’ll come back to this point later, but Silver Bullets are great for helping us solve problems that don’t exist.

## “Only good guys use silver bullets”

Only good guys use silver bullets, which is a problem when we think we’re the good guy.

And sometimes we don’t know enough yet to make that decision.

The Spider - “The King of Crooks” started off as a villain in British comics. But that kind of limited the writing a little, so in many of the later stories he became a Super Hero. Our opinions change.

But we tend to “know it all” when we often “know very little”

People ask me all the time over email. I want to write a framework to make WebDrive Easy to use, where should I start. I find it pretty easy to use, They get the answer - maybe you should learn to use it first. Instead of learning the tool, they want to create a Silver Bullet - but they don’t know how.

So this is where we start to introduce more caution into the hype.

Do we buy the latest, greatest, do it all automation tool when we are experts in a particular tool? Or do we do it when we don’t even know where to start, where we have very little experience in the world of automation, and when we pay attention to experts about keyword driven and ROI. When we don’t even know how to make automation part of our test process?

Too often we make these decisions when we know the least. A good sales pitch will easily fill the vacuum of our knowledge.

## “You can only hurt werewolves with a silver bullet”

This is why I don’t go werewolf hunting. Because I don’t have a gun, and don’t own any silver bullets.

And the fact that werewolves don’t exist.

So to lower the hype level a little further.

But what I love about “Werewolves” and “Silver Bullets” is that is a pretty focussed solution. As a tool it does one thing, and it does it pretty well. There is a need for the wielder to have skill, they have to be able to aim, they have to be able to fire a gun and hit a target, and fight through to the end game to get to the werewolf.

Its not an automated tank that anyone can use to slay the pesky werewolves, it is a targeted solution that requires skill.

## “Reification &amp; Nominalisation”

Reification is the thingification of abstract ideas - “they don’t have enough democracy”

I first encountered this concept when studying psychotherapy, because some of the therapy processes focus on nominalisation as a target. Nominalisation is when verbs become nouns.

“I don’t get enough respect” Its a nominalisation, you could put the ‘respect’ in a wheelbarrow. You could count how much ‘respect’ is in the wheelbarrow. But of course you can’t. And you can’t deal with the problem when it is phrased in this way because there is some sort of equivalence going on. Some actions over here, some glances over here, some words over here, all of that adds up to ‘not enough respect’.

We have to decompose and analyse problems so we can fix them. And very often I perceive Silver Bullets in terms of Nominialisation and Reification.

The Respect thing. Did you ever hear the test community say that “Testing doesn’t get enough respect” - What? How? How much is enough? Who’s not getting it, I’m getting respect? This is the kind of nonsense that contributed to the huge increase in certification. People respect people with qualifications, testing doesn’t have any qualifications, if we want respect we must get qualified and certified”

The same thing can happen with automation. We don’t have enough automation, our automation isn’t good enough. But the danger is. That there are obvious products from the process, the verb of automaton. Like likes of code, things that are reported as tests, pass fail values, easy coverage metrics that we can apply. We can count the output. So we think we can count the Nominalisation. But we can’t. The number of passing automated tests is not the value of your automation process. It is the measure of your nominalisation, and that is a shallow place to measure. And leads to a lot of ROI nonsense.

But this is a human failing. A lot of Silver Bullet stuff. Is simple human failing. so I suggest we keep our “Silver Bulllets” as ‘ideals’ We keep our processes (like test automation) as verbs, not nouns. And then Silver Bullets become things we aspire to, but never reach, and we won’t be tempted to buy them.

## “I started my career building Silver Bullets”

Having said that, if we didn’t buy them, I wouldn’t have a career.

In terms of my background I was essentially built to quest for Silver Bullets. I started my career in CASE tools, Computer Aided Software Engineering tools, where we would 'draw' programs to avoid coding errors,

At University I thought that the best way to solve the problems in the Industry was to adopt CASE tools. But this is what we were taught. University teaches ‘best practices’ to people who can’t evaluate their ‘best ness’ and we assume that the lecturers actually know ‘best’. But they don’t. Very often University is about ‘interesting’ rather than ‘practical.

CASE tools. Model tools. prevented the kind of errors that university students were making - simple logic errors, syntax errors. And remember this was in the time when they were costly because you sometimes didn’t have access to the keyboard, and other people typed in your stuff. And we didn’t have IDEs.

So today, a lot of the solutions to the problems we faced are IDEs, syntax checkers, cheap computers, open source software, unit test frameworks. Not, big heavy weight diagramatical tools.

At University. Before I had been out in the real world. When I knew very little. I thought I knew a lot. And so I built a Silver Bullet.

I wrote one with a COBOL interpreter built in so that we could execute the diagrams rather than generate code.

After University I went to work for a company in Glasgow to build this properly. The company I worked

for went bankrupt. About a month after I started.

Before I even started in Software Testing I was building Silver Bullets.

## “Lesson Learned: Never build a generic ‘Silver Bullet’ tool for others to use”

And right at the start of my quest I was building Silver Bullets for other people to sell.

I started working with a consultancy company, building Silver Bullets for them. This time a CAST (Computer Aided Software Testing) tool for SSADM. This was the clumsiest tool ever. The company I worked for went

bankrupt.

I moved on to writing another CAST tool for a spin off company - this was in the days before TestDirector had really taken over the world so Test Process tools were in vogue. There was clearly money to be made by writing a generic tool that would solve everyone’s test management and modelling problems.

I was programming rather than designing the tool and though I questioned some of the design decisions they weren't changed. The company I worked for went bankrupt.

Really if you do build a generic tool. You need to somehow get it mandated. So that you don’t really solve problems you provide a process so that managers that don’t know what they are doing don’t have to worry about that. They just buy the tool. Buy the process. And have someone else pay for all the follow on problems that happen as a result.

## “You can learn a lot by making Silver Bullets”

Over this I learned a lot about modelling tools and processes. I learned to pursue the highest level of abstraction possible in our modelling, at the highest level appropriate for my domain. This has a knock on effect on test automation abstraction layers but I didn't see the relevance until later.

See now I refactor. When I need to. And it does seem to confuse people. It happens all the time. People want to build frameworks that do all the logging and screenshots, and abstract scripts and keywords, and data generation and etc. etc.

Why? It isn’t to solve a problem, because most of those solutions already exist in the programming language they are using.

It is sometimes to solve a projected problem. They see that other people are not using those libraries so what they need is a framework that pulls in the “best of breed” tools. But they are only “best of breed” for the person making the framework. Other people are happy with their tools. So will never use the framework. And those that don’t use the other libraries, won’t use the framework anyway.

I've done a lot of automation. Automation has its share of Silver Bullets. Some are proprietary and you risk the tool becoming your process. But open source tools are not immune to the lure of the Silver Bullet. People 'build on' the tools. e.g. people use WebDriver, but think their testers can't learn to code so they build a Silver Bullet to let their testers do automation without understanding. I have examples of tools that sit on top of other tools to provide a 'simpler' view of the underlying tool domain, but limit the capabilities of the other tool and impact the ability to keep up to date with the underlying tool changes. But they don't model the problem domain, because they tried to make a generic model across teams and companies. There are other approaches by learning how to model DSLs at the correct domain, by understanding how to model the 'process' and not avoid knowledge sharing and attempting to maintain the level of ignorance in the test team.

We need to stamp out ignorance and credit people with the ability to learn.

And most of that is ‘justification’. People build generic frameworks because they want to. Because the act of building a framework is intellectually challenging. There might be a chance for fame and fortune. But ultimately it satisfies them. Which is fine. You can learn a lot by making Silver bullets.

But you’ll learn more when you realise they fail to do what you justified them as being able to do. And it can take a lot of time and money to find that out. So I caution you “don’t do it”

I learned to simplify my modelling techniques so I have a set of high level abstract models and approaches. I learned to value 'views' of domains: Entity, List, Hierarchy, Graph. I can apply these modelling views to other people’s tools to learn how to use their tools in ways they never figured out that they might be used for.

Sometimes I feel like Willy Wonka when I say that “Don’t drink from the Chocolate river” “Don’t eat the bubble gum” “Don’t sit there” “Don’t go down there”. People aren’t going to listen. Silver Bullets, quick wins and generic solutions are too attractive.

## “Sometimes the bad guy thinks he’s the good guy”

“Hands up everyone that knows best” Or has known best.

Sometimes you do. Sometimes we don’t get to see the problem through.

He’s building a tool for everyone to use.

When I take over the world and put everyone into subservient slavery and hypnotise them to my will and do things my way. Only then can they be happy. Sometimes the bad guy thinks he’s the good guy.

You have to be aware that we all have this tendency.

You don’t set out to be the bad guy. But we shouldn’t turn our specific solutions into generic solutions. And we shouldn’t try and force generic solutions into specific holes.

Because sometimes your generic solution has overhead that they don’t need. “You have to cross reference every test case to a requirement”, “You have to write a script for every test you want to run, otherwise we can’t mark it as pass or fail”

And it doesn’t matter how incredulous it seems to the specific project. The Bad Guy pushing the generic overhead thinks he is the good guy and that everyone on the ground doesn’t know what they are doing.

## “Other People’s Silver Bullets are like a black hole”

You have to be really careful bringing other people’s Silver Bullets into your world.

You have to make sure they match what you do. That they don’t mandate things that you don’t need to do. And that they don’t limit what you do.

Try and live with a lighter weight solution if you can. And always have a migration path, or fall back strategy so that you can Run Away.

## “Other People’s Silver Bullets can hurt”

Otherwise you’re going to get hurt.

If you let the tool or the process, or whatever else you have brought in as the latest “Silver Bullet” and if you have to tailor your context around that tool, rather than absorbing it in to your context seamlessly. They will hurt.

And they hurt more when they don’t work.

Morale will drop. You won’t be able to justify the tool based on increased productivity, you have to justify it based on metrics that it produces, or calls to ‘best practice’

## “Lesson Learned: NEVER let tools limit your testing.”

Never, EVER.

This is a microphone in my office. I wanted a boom mike, and I needed it now. But all I had was a desktop mike. So I macguyered up a boom mike.

I could have said that I “can’t do the job”. I could have “waited until I had the right tool”. Instead I augmented what I had.

## “Bypass Silver Bullet Constraints”

I learned how to bypass the constraints of other people's Silver Bullets.

When I started testing properly and had to use other people's tools. I learned how to bypass every tool so that I never let tools limit my process. A lot of people don't design their test process, they use the tool, and the use of the tool becomes the process. Hence the reason we have 'thou shalt write a script before thy does any testing' processes, because the tools enforce this. I would figure out, regardless of the tool, how to minimise the impact it had on my process.

I wrote code to write directly into the tool databases and create scripts automatically, I wrote scripts to pull metrics out of tools that the tools didn't support - to allow me to automate the reporting that other people wanted and I saw no value in. I wrote front ends for other tools which worked off text files rather than GUIs to make my work process more efficient. I can elaborate with specific examples the strategies I adopt to do this.

I remember trying to tell these experiences at conferences years ago, when I was still starting out, but no-one accepted the talks - perhaps tool vendors had a tight grip of conferences then?

Augment other tools, and use them in combination. Don't build the 'one tool to rule them all'

I then started to Augment other tools. A lot of test teams get hung up on tools and can't distinguish between 'tool' and 'process'. As a concrete example when companies use 'Jira' people can't figure out how to use 'Jira' for their testing so they want to use a 'test tool' and interface with Jira, since they don't know how to model their process or tool usage, they end up using the 'test tool' as their process, then tag on waste by duplicating information in 'Jira'. If the company has Jira then I can create a test process around it by modelling the Entities, the list views I need, the relationship Graphs I want to maintain. If the company has 'Trac' then I can... do the same thing. If the company has 'Mingle' then I can ... do the same thing.

A result of all of this is that I've written scripts to pull exploratory testing reports out of Jira and combine them into daily reports that span projects to meet the reporting needs of myself and others, all without impacting the process of the individual testers.

## “The lone ranger uses Silver Bullets as a last resort”

Tools change all the time, the list is not important. Knowing why you need them and how to find them, those skills I value. I value the use of weapons, and the strategies of war. Not the stockpiling of armaments.

Lesson Learned: Change your definition of tool

Lesson Learned: Use tools to Augment your testing

Lesson Learned: Don't list 'Who' and 'What' list 'Why' and 'How'

## “The lone Ranger’s gun holds six silver bullets”

I wanted to build an armoury.

Most of my test tools are not test tools. I spent years collating lists of tools. I've spoken about this before

<http://www.compendiumdev.co.uk/page.php?title=alternative_test_tools_in_action>

but I've learned from this. The collation is not the point, the 'knowing' lots of tools to use is not the point.

I learned the generic strategies that I abstract from this, that makes my testing stronger.

I want tools that help me observe, inspect, manipulate.

When I talk about tools and technical testing to people they still want 'lists of tools', I can understand why, but we need to move on from that.

We need to drop tools when we don't use them. We don't maintain an inventory. We use what we have to hand, guided by our knowledge of what we need to fight the next battle.

## “The Lone Ranger didn’t make his own Silver Bullets... Tonto Did”

We need to find a way of becoming self sufficient if we want the requisite variety to avoid Silver Bullet Black holes. If you’re lucky you’ll have Tonto, but then you are vulnerable to his definition of variety.

So you can’t blame Tonto if he goes the back way round the house, and kills the bad guy shooting at you before you have a chance to question him. If you don’t exhibit the requisite variety to adopt that strategy.

## “The Lone Ranger’s Silver Bullets remind him of the value of a human life”

This isn't necessarily an easy message to hear.

The tool is not the power, the wielder and how well they wield is the power.

So we have to recognise the value of human life in our processes. Allow people the space and responsibility to adapt, and use their ingenuity.

Don’t tie them down to mandated processes and actions that are forced on you by adopting a tool, which you thought was your Silver Bullet but clearly wasn’t.

I worked in an environment using a test management tool. One of those that tracks execution in terms of scripts executed. And you can only raise a defect if it is attached to a Script, and a script needs a test, and a test needs to be cross referenced to a requirement, and a requirement needs a set of test conditions that have to be covered by a test case.

And I didn’t have time to do all that. So I build a graph, and automatically generated paths, and each path was a test case and and I could automatically generate the script. And because it was all automatically generated I could achieve a lot of documented coverage, very quickly.

And what did we learn from that? Automation? Reducing the overhead? No, we learned that everyone had to create a graph, and manually add all the paths into the tool, and cross reference and … Aaargh.

When tools only help the management get numbers without speaking to staff, they are not a Silver Bullet, they are some sort of bludgeon. A stick you hit people with.

Value the life and time of the people that work for you. Value their ability to come up with new ideas.

## “The most dangerous part of a quest is when you believe you have reached the end”

The quest is good, we journey and we learn. The belief that we have

found 'The' Silver Bullet is dangerous.

This was a problem for King Arthur. When they were hunting the holy grail, another “Silver Bullet”. How would they know they had found the correct Grail?

Fortunately Arthur had Merlin. And he made a chair. The Seige Perlous. A seat at the round table destined for the knight who returned with the holy grail. And if any knight returned with the holy grail, he would sit at the Seige Perilous and much feasting and rejoicing would occur.

But, if he sat at the Seige Perilous without the true grail. Then he would DIE.

This is what you need for tool vendors when they come to your organisation.

Sit them in the Seige Perilous and you will get instant feedback on the efficacy of the next “Silver Bullet”

But we don’t have that luxury of instant feedback. But we do need feedback. So we have to do everything we can to get feedback, and I don’t mean metrics. I mean feedback, information, on how well we are meeting our Aims.

Because the point of a “Silver Bullet” is the quest, knowing what you are aiming for and striving for it. And on the quest you will try many things, each of which you think will add value, and “might be the one”. But it won’t be. So we have to recognise that, and learn its weaknesses fast, and augment them, and keep looking.

The danger happens if we think we have found it.

## “Keep Looking”

A few Lessons learned:

\- Never build a generic 'Silver Bullet' tool, ever

\- Never let tools limit your testing

\- Never build 'on top' of another tool

\- Augment other tools, and use them in combination, use their APIs if possible

\- Use what you have first, assume you can use it differently

\- Stamp out ignorance, challenge people to learn

\- model at the problem domain, not the physical domain

\- Generic models are a side-effect, not a goal
